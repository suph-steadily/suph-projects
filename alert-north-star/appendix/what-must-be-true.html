What keeping the alert costs

Homes over 100 years old cannot bind until an underwriter reviews them. It is our first and most common blocking alert. Per 100 quotes hitting it, 63 die with nobody ever looking, about 16 bind, and 4.5% of those cancel within 90 days. Keeping the gate costs about 2,200 forced underwriter reviews per month. What that buys is the question.

The twin already ran the experiment

We do not need to guess what removal does. Homes aged 91 to 100 bind today with no alert and no review: about 18 of 100 bind and 6.5% cancel within 90 days, against about 16 and 4.5% when reviewed. Reading the full 101-and-older book against that twin yields the monthly ledger.

Why the decision stalls

The ledger's two sides share no unit, and we do not hold the prices that would convert them: what one bind is worth over its life, or what one NOC costs all-in. Underwriting answers for every NOC, so zero new NOCs is the honest position from that seat. Growth answers for every bind, so taking all 115 is honest from its seat. Both are right from where they sit. The same collision ends every attempt to retire any alert, so nothing ever retires. The problem is not dwelling age. It is the missing instrument.

The weighing machine

The recommendation is the weighing machine: one instrument, two halves. The first half forecasts the counts, how many binds, NOCs, and corrective endorsements removal adds or removes. That half mostly exists; it produced the ledger above.

The second half is the one we do not have: the weights themselves. What is a bind worth, once retention and the GWP growth it compounds are counted? What does a NOC cost, when the worst case is not the lost policy but the lost agent? What does a corrective endorsement weigh? What are 2,200 freed reviews worth in underwriter time? None of these is a number we hold today, and until every count carries a weight, the beam reads nothing.

And the machine is not a dwelling age tool. It prices any alert, in both directions: an existing alert proposed for retirement, and every future alert before it is allowed to ship.

How real is this?

This is not a platform pitch. The counting half has already run once, by hand: dwelling age is its output, and the ledger above is what it produces. And one pricing module already runs as code: Darren's agent-production calculator takes any proposal as a change in alert, NOC, and cure rates and returns a signed production number, with honest brackets where a price is uncertain. What remains unpriced is narrower than it was a week ago: lifetime bind value, the freed-review hour, the customer side of a NOC, and the loss join. Pricing those is the ask, and it is a finance and actuarial partnership, not a data pull.

5~1 yr lag

What a live test cannot prove

A live test cannot make this decision. It reads the fast numbers inside 90 days: bind lift, NOCs, corrective endorsements. Realized loss ratio takes about a year, and no method today ties an alert to actual losses. The answer sits in data we already own: the twin has bound unreviewed for years, and its losses are on the books. Backtesting those losses against the reviewed 101-and-older book, with actuarial owning the method, prices the loss side without the wait. The test keeps exactly one job: validating the fast numbers behind a reversible flag, 7 states, random 50% holdout, 30 to 60 days, inside kill bounds underwriting sets.

Shrinking the cost pan

The review's work decomposes. About half of approvals change nothing; real judgment is about 11%. The rest is three nameable jobs, each with a move that shifts weight off the cost pan; the scale re-weighs the trade after every move.

A
Widen the roof rule
One in four approvals ends in a hand-applied roof surfacing exclusion, 85% with no roof fact changed. Live automation fires on roughly the worst 4%; underwriters hand-apply on 21.3%. Widening costs about two extra exclusions per catch at roughly $0 premium relief; underwriting picks the threshold.
B
Buy better pre-fill data
Six property fields carry 90% of data corrections, and 65 to 79% of underwriter field fixes repair vendor pre-fill the agent never touched. Better upstream data shrinks both the fixes and the premium at risk.
C
Consume the Pluribus checks
Commercial use, historic districts, and unit-count issues are 82% of rejections. Pluribus ships automatic checks for exactly these around October; consuming them moves most rejection work off the review desk.

A fourth lever cuts across the last two: ask the agent in the flow. A direct question (historic district, commercial use, unit count) ships as a form field months before detection lands, and an answered question puts the risk on the record even when the answer is wrong. It is not free, and the machine weighs it like everything else: every added question is a small alert paid in conversion on every quote, and where agents type over our pre-fill today, underwriters end up correcting those values 2 to 6 times more often than the untouched feed. Ask where the representation itself matters; detect where data can.

Darren's curves add a fifth lever: raise the cure rate. It is the one dial in his calculator that wins under every price assumption, and it lightens the cost pan without touching the alert.

The roughly 11% needing real judgment and the roughly 2% genuinely about age (pre-1940 wiring, visible decay), kept as a narrow rule or priced risk, never automate.

The North Star

Dwelling age is test case one, not the destination. The point is running the machine on every alert we have and every alert anyone proposes, until no alert survives that a system could have handled and none ships unweighed. Thursday's ask is help pricing both pans, not permission. Underwriting sets the kill bounds, actuarial owns the loss backtest, and Darren and Christine make the call, as a sized trade.

Before you retell it · three numbers to say right

Bare removal is not free upside. The 25 added NOCs per month is a floor: NOC rates climb 22.8% per decade of dwelling age, the 101-and-older book averages 117 years, and an unreviewed book projects 7 to 11 NOCs per 100 binds, above the twin's 6.5.

Two NOC rates are now in circulation; name the slice. The 6.5 per 100 on this page is inspection-driven NOCs on the twin cohort. Darren's 11.7% is every UW NOC, book-wide, within 90 days. Both are right; quoting one against the other is how a room gets lost.

Premium at risk is about $26K per month, deliberately bound-only. Underwriter fixes raise price 3.4 times more often than they lower it, so unreviewed binds under-collect on policies we keep. A larger all-approved figure exists, but it misleads: that premium mostly never binds.
